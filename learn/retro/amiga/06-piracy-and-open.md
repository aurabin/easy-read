# Lesson 6: Software piracy and open software

**Time:** 15 minutes. **You need:** lesson 1 of the Amiga course.

## Short answer

Software piracy is copying or sharing software without permission.
It is usually against the law.
Open software is different. The maker gives you permission in writing.
This whole project is open. You may use it, change it, and share it.

## What piracy means

Piracy is:

- copying a program and giving it to others;
- downloading a paid program for free from a stranger;
- sharing ROM files or Workbench disks you do not own;
- removing a licence key and passing it on.

It does not matter if you do not charge money.
Sharing a copy for free can still be piracy.

## Why it was common on old machines

Old computers had no app stores. Software cost a lot of money.
Many games had copy protection. People cracked that protection.
Cracked copies spread through schools and clubs.
People shared disks the way we now share links.

That was the culture. It was still mostly against the law.

### The "abandonware" myth

A company may stop selling an old program.
The copyright does not disappear. It can last for decades.
"Nobody sells it now" does not make copying legal.
A ROM or Workbench disk is still copyrighted today.

## The law, in plain words

- Software has an owner. The owner holds the copyright.
- You may use a copy you bought legally.
- You may not copy it for strangers without permission.
- A licence is permission. Read it. It can say yes or no.
- Rules differ by country. This is not legal advice.

## The ethics

Makers need to be paid. That is how they keep making things.
Piracy takes that payment away. Small makers are hurt most.

There is a real access problem too. Old machines are rare.
Some companies will not sell old software any more.
Preservation matters. But piracy is not the same as preservation.
You can preserve history in legal ways. Ask permission. Use open replacements.
Keep copies you own.

## Cracks and the demoscene are not the same

On the Amiga there were two different groups.

- **Crackers** removed copy protection and released cracked games.
- **Demo groups** made art, music, and code just to show off.

The demoscene gave us the Boing Ball and countless demos.
That is original creative work. It is not the same as piracy.
Many demo groups asked people to buy the real games.

## The open answer

Open software solves the problem. The maker gives permission up front.

- **Open licence:** MIT, GPL, Apache for code. CC0 or CC BY for art and text.
- **Free and open systems:** AROS is an open system for Amiga computers.
- **Free emulators:** WinUAE and FS-UAE are free to use.
- **Homebrew:** new software made for old machines, often free.
- **Public domain:** works with no copyright restrictions.

When software is open, you may copy it. You may share it. That is the point.

## This project is completely open

- Code: MIT licence.
- Art, music, text: CC0 / CC BY.
- Default Amiga system: open (AROS-style). No proprietary ROM needed.
- We do not include copyrighted ROM or Workbench files.
- The requester records just the name. It is a file you already own.
- In the virtual fields, everybody is an admin. No login.

We give you the right to use, change, and share this course.

## If you want the real thing

- Buy or borrow a legal copy of the software.
- Use a ROM you own, with a real emulator.
- Use a free open system such as AROS instead.
- Check the licence before you copy anything.

## Red flags

- "Free full game, no payment" from a stranger.
- "All ROMs in one pack" — that is a piracy pack.
- "Abandonware, so it is legal" — usually false.
- Someone asking you to share files you do not own.

## Try this

2. Find its licence. Is it proprietary or open?
3. Find one open alternative for it.
4. Say one sentence: why is open software easier to share legally?
